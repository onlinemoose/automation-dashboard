# Style (bundled default — replaced wholesale via house_style)

This is the module's default voice. An operator can replace this whole
file with their own `house_style`; when they do, none of it survives —
their string is the style. The hard invariants in each stage's
`standards.md` still hold regardless, and an explicit `tone` overrides
this file for register.

## Language

British English unless a supplied `house_style` says otherwise: spelling
("colour", "organise", "-ise" not "-ize"), grammar, and idiom. Dates in
prose as 4 March 2025. Single quotation marks, double only inside them.
Never American English by default, under any circumstances.

## Voice

Clear, direct, and specific. A professional register unless `tone` says otherwise. State what is true rather than what is missing. Prefer the active voice and name who acts. Let the substance carry the point — no heroic or breathless language, no throat-clearing.

Register: observation, not immersion

The default register for this module is diagnostic and observational — naming a pattern the reader can recognise and check against their own case, not narrating a scene the reader is presumed to be living in. The test is not grammatical person; it's whether a sentence asserts the reader's specific felt experience as fact, or names a pattern with acknowledgement of its own evidential basis.

Fits the register:
"You are struggling with X."
"Meetings where teams don't speak up are difficult — evidence suggests many teams struggle with this."
"Postmortems that seek blame and finger-pointing can be difficult to work on."

Breaks the register:
"You landed into the room, facing silence." — an invented, specific scene asserted as the reader's own experience. Presumptuous, not diagnostic.

This is the same principle behind two rules elsewhere in this pipeline: never asserting a scene as the reader's lived experience, and building insight moments from grounded explanation rather than decorative narration.

## Avoid by default

An operator's `house_style` may permit any of these; the bundled default
does not.

- Em dashes as a clause connector. Use a full stop, a comma, or
  parentheses instead. En dashes remain correct for number and date
  ranges (2020–2024); hyphens for compounds (cross-team, well-known).
- The theatrical reversal "X isn't A, it's B" as a rhetorical reveal —
  especially stacked with a metaphor ("a blind spot wearing the costume
  of confidence") or a "less, not more" tail. Assert B directly. A
  plain qualifier ("needs structure, not just goodwill") is fine.
- "not just X, but Y" as a rhetorical frame.
- Narrating the piece's own structure, or telling the reader how to
  regard a passage: "here's the turn", "the turn most people miss",
  "here's the thing", "here's the mechanism", "here's why this matters",
  "this is the mechanism worth sitting with", "it's worth dwelling on
  this". Make the point without naming the seam or coaching the
  reaction.
- Vague "quiet", "silent", "unnoticed" used to imply something has
  slipped past the reader. Say what is actually happening.
- Decorative invented specifics and metaphors that carry no information: an "edge case someone hit last Tuesday," an over-the-shoulder image, a costume/mask metaphor, or an asserted scene of the reader's own experience they did not describe ("you landed into the room, facing silence"). State the thing plainly, or name the pattern rather than asserting a scene for them.
- "it's important to note that", "it's worth noting", and similar filler
  throat-clearing.
- Throat-clearing openers: "in today's fast-paced world", "in an era
  of", "now more than ever".
- The rule-of-three list used as a crutch ("faster, cleaner, simpler").
- "moreover" / "furthermore" as paragraph openers.
- Marketing-copy filler where a precise word exists: leverage, utilise,
  spearhead, passionate, driven, dynamic, synergy, seamless,
  cutting-edge, deep dive, circle back, game-changer, best-in-class.
